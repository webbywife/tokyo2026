On the forecast: each day below shows live numbers for that day's actual destination — not just central Tokyo. Mid-September in the Kantō region is still summer: hot and humid near the coast (~31 °C), several degrees cooler up in Hakone and Nikkō, and it's the tail of typhoon season, so a passing storm can suspend the Odakyū, Tōbu, or Enoden lines with little warning.
You've done Hakone, Nikkō & Yokohama — so the day trips are forks. Each of Days 1–3 offers a choice; pick on the morning you land, by the 3-day forecast:
  • Day 1 (arrival): a short ease-in near Shinjuku — the Gyoen/Meiji circle, Kawagoe, Nakano, Kōenji, teamLab, Minka-en, or back to Yokohama.
  • Day 2: Okutama (limestone cave + river gorge — the cave is weatherproof) · Nokogiriyama (cliff lookout + giant Buddha — needs a calm bay) · or Hakone again.
  • Day 3: Mito + Hitachi Seaside Park (great garden + kochia coast — wants a dry day) · Nokogiriyama (if not done Day 2) · or Nikkō again.
  • Day 4 stays Kamakura + Enoshima — it has to be short and close for the 1 a.m. flight — with Kawagoe or Yokohama as lighter fallbacks.
  • Slot the wind-sensitive one (Nokogiriyama's ropeway, or Hakone's ropeway/boat) on the calmest day; put Mito on the driest; take Okutama on the wettest of the three.
More swaps, if none of the above appeals — all with far fewer foreign tourists than the classics:
  • Chichibu (Saitama) — Seibu Laview express ~80 min. Nagatoro gorge river-boats, Chichibu Shrine, Hitsujiyama Park, three walkable sake breweries. Rain: boats stop in high water; the rest is fine.
  • Sawara + Katori Jingū (Chiba) — JR ~90 min. An Edo canal town of willow-lined water and little boat rides, next to a vast ancient forested shrine that almost no one visits. Rain: a street stroll, umbrella OK.
  • Mt Takao — Keiō line ~50 min, ¥390. Cable car, a slope temple, easy hikes with Fuji from the top on a clear day. Locals only midweek. Rain: temple + cable car OK, summit not worth it.
  • Kawagoe ("Little Edo," Saitama) — ~40 min from Shinjuku. Kurazukuri warehouse street, a bell tower, candy alley, 540 stone rakan at Kita-in. A relaxed half-day (also a Day 1 and Day 4 option). Rain: mostly covered.
  • Ōyama (Isehara, Kanagawa) — Odakyū ~70 min + bus. A cable car up to Afuri Shrine and Ōyama-dera on a sacred hiking mountain, tofu the local speciality — a proper pilgrimage almost no foreign tourist does. Rain: cable car + shrines OK, the ridge hike not.

Why it's built this way: Okutama is the closest place to Tokyo that actually feels rural and cool — 90 minutes of Chūō-line commuters, then a limestone cave that ignores the weather, a river gorge, and a reservoir lake, with barely a foreign face all day. A real reset between the coast and the city.
Possible hiccups:
  • Ōme-line trains past Ōme run every 30–60 min and the last useful one back is mid-evening — photograph the return timetable at Okutama Station before you head up to the caves.
  • The Nippara road is single-lane; the bus fills on a fine weekend and forms a queue — take the first one after you arrive.
  • Okutama is cash country — draw money at Shinjuku before you leave; the ATMs up here are few and close early.
  • In heavy rain the gorge trail and the dam bridge shut and the river browns — the cave plus a wasabi-soba lunch still make a half-day, then head back early.
  • Weekend Nokogiriyama alternative: the Tokyo-Bay ferry stops running in strong wind — check the sailing before you commit to the ferry route rather than the train.

Possible hiccups:
  • Sunday makes Kamakura busy and the Enoden slow — start at Tsurugaoka Hachimangū by 09:00 and you'll be ahead of the worst of it.
  • The Enoden runs in rain but suspends in typhoon-strength wind; if the coast forecast is bad, swap this day for the Rain-plan tab and take one of the other trips on a calmer slot earlier in the week.
  • The last Airport Limousine buses from Shinjuku leave ~22:00–22:30 — if the day runs long, switch to the Shinagawa → Keikyū train, which runs later.

When to reach for this: a typhoon or a wash-out day. If a typhoon actually lands:
  • Don't travel. Above-ground JR lines and every day-trip railway stop; the Tokyo Metro usually keeps a reduced service but stations flood-gate and crowds surge.
  • Stock the conbini the night before — bread, water, snacks — and treat it as a hotel day: laundry, planning, a long bath downstairs.
  • Reserved-seat fares (Romancecar, SPACIA) for a cancelled train refund at the station; the area passes refund if unactivated. Rebook the trip for the next clear day.
  • Flights out of Haneda can slip a day either side of a direct hit — keep an eye on PR 422's status and the airline app.

Where it goes

Heads up: the four area passes (~$121 together) are the single biggest block after airfare and the hotel — but each one replaces $20–40 of separate train tickets, so they mostly pay for themselves the moment you make more than two hops. The $25 "weather slack" is real: in mid-September assume one trip gets rearranged. Refundable passes and station refunds cover most of it, but a non-refundable reserved seat is ~$8–16 gone.
Overall: mid-September Kantō is Manila-humid at the coast and jacket-cool up the mountains — breathable cotton for Yokohama and Kamakura, one real layer for Hakone and Nikkō. A packable rain jacket beats an umbrella on the day trips (wind), sunscreen for the between-shower glare, grippy shoes for wet temple steps. Watch the typhoon track and keep the tab order loose.
