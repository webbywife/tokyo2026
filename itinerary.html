On the forecast: each day below shows live numbers for that day's actual destination — not just central Tokyo. Mid-September in the Kantō region is still summer: hot and humid near the coast (~31 °C), several degrees cooler up in Hakone and Nikkō, and it's the tail of typhoon season, so a passing storm can suspend the Odakyū, Tōbu, or Enoden lines with little warning.
Read the sky first — the tab order is only a default. The morning you land, check the 3-day forecast and assign the trips to the days:
  • Clearest, calmest day → Hakone. The ropeway and the lake boat are the most weather-sensitive things on the whole trip, and any Fuji chance needs clear air.
  • A settled, non-stormy day → Nikkō. The shrine complex is fine in light rain, but the mountain buses up to Kegon Falls and Lake Chūzenji stop first when weather turns.
  • Light rain is OK → Kamakura + Enoshima. Temples, the covered Komachi-dōri, the Iwaya sea caves and the aquarium all work damp — and it's the easiest day to cut short for the airport.
  • Worst day → Yokohama, or the Rain-plan tab. Both are almost entirely indoors.
Quieter whole-day swaps — trade one of the four for somewhere with far fewer foreign tourists:
  • Chichibu (Saitama) — Seibu Laview express ~80 min from Ikebukuro. Nagatoro gorge river-boats, Chichibu Shrine, Hitsujiyama Park, three walkable sake breweries. Rain: boats stop in high water; the rest is fine.
  • Okutama (still Tokyo) — JR Ōme line ~2 h. The Nippara limestone caves stay 11 °C inside — an all-weather and hot-day pick — plus the Hatonosu gorge trail and Lake Okutama. Rain: caves fine, trails not.
  • Sawara + Katori Jingū (Chiba) — JR ~90 min. An Edo canal town of willow-lined water and little boat rides, next to a vast ancient forested shrine that almost no one visits. Rain: a street stroll, umbrella OK.
  • Mt Takao — Keiō line ~50 min, ¥390. Cable car, a temple on the slope, easy hikes with Fuji from the top on a clear day. Locals only midweek. Rain: temple + cable car OK, summit not worth it.
  • Kawagoe ("Little Edo," Saitama) — ~40 min from Shinjuku. Kurazukuri warehouse street, a bell tower, candy alley, 540 stone rakan at Kita-in. A relaxed half-day. Rain: mostly covered.
  • Nokogiriyama (Bōsō, Chiba) — train + a Tokyo-Bay ferry. A cliff-edge "Hell Lookout," a 31 m Buddha bigger than Kamakura's, 1,500 rakan carved into the hillside — and empty. Rain/wind: ropeway stops; skip on a bad day.
  • Mito – Kairakuen (Ibaraki) — limited express ~75 min from Ueno. One of Japan's three great gardens, uncrowded next to Kanazawa's Kenrokuen; pair it with Hitachi Seaside Park, where the kochia hills turn crimson from late September. Rain: gardens and a park — better dry.
  • Ōyama (Isehara, Kanagawa) — Odakyū ~70 min + bus. A cable car up to Afuri Shrine and Ōyama-dera on a sacred hiking mountain, with tofu as the local speciality — a proper pilgrimage almost no foreign tourist does. Rain: cable car + shrines OK, the ridge hike not.

Why it's worth it: Kamakura's Great Buddha and hill temples, the anime-famous Enoden coast run, and Enoshima's shrine, tower and sea caves — a full day out that's still only an hour from Shinjuku, so a late start or a passing storm just trims the edges. Possible hiccups:
  • Sunday makes Kamakura busy and the Enoden slow — start at Tsurugaoka Hachimangū by 09:00 and you'll be ahead of the worst of it.
  • The Enoden runs in rain but suspends in typhoon-strength wind; if the coast forecast is bad, swap this day for the Rain-plan tab and take one of the other trips on a calmer slot earlier in the week.
  • The last Airport Limousine buses from Shinjuku leave ~22:00–22:30 — if the day runs long, switch to the Shinagawa → Keikyū train, which runs later.
  • A 01:00 departure means Haneda's shops and some restaurants are shut — eat before you leave the city, or rely on Terminal 3's 24-hour options.

Weatherproof swaps

When to reach for this: a typhoon or a wash-out day. Mid-September Japan sees several named storms a month brushing the coast; when one is close, the day-trip railways (Odakyū, Tōbu, Enoden) suspend before the Tokyo metro does. Check tenki.jp and the JR East / Odakyū / Tōbu service-alert pages the night before, front-load Hakone and Nikkō onto the clearest days, and drop one of these into the bad one.

If a typhoon actually lands:
  • Don't travel. Above-ground JR lines and every day-trip railway stop; the Tokyo Metro usually keeps a reduced service but stations flood-gate and crowds surge.
  • Stock the conbini the night before — bread, water, snacks — and treat it as a hotel day: laundry, planning, a long bath downstairs.
  • Reserved-seat fares (Romancecar, SPACIA) for a cancelled train refund at the station; the area passes refund if unactivated. Rebook the trip for the next clear day.
  • Flights out of Haneda can slip a day either side of a direct hit — keep an eye on PR 422's status and the airline app.

Where it goes

Heads up: the four area passes (~$121 together) are the single biggest block after airfare and the hotel — but each one replaces $20–40 of separate train tickets, so they mostly pay for themselves the moment you make more than two hops. The $25 "weather slack" is real: in mid-September assume one trip gets rearranged. Refundable passes and station refunds cover most of it, but a non-refundable reserved seat is ~$8–16 gone.
Overall: mid-September Kantō is Manila-humid at the coast and jacket-cool up the mountains — breathable cotton for Yokohama and Kamakura, one real layer for Hakone and Nikkō. A packable rain jacket beats an umbrella on the day trips (wind), sunscreen for the between-shower glare, grippy shoes for wet temple steps. Watch the typhoon track and keep the tab order loose.
